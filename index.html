Childhood immunisation in Australia: how close is coverage to the 95% target?

National coverage: how close is Australia to the 95% target?

Australia's national target is 95% childhood immunisation coverage. This visualisation examines how close Australia is to that target, how coverage has changed over time, and whether patterns differ by age, vaccine type, state, Primary Health Network, and population group.

Age-five coverage sits just below the 95% target

In 2024, 93.63% of five-year-olds were vaccinated. Only the ACT, at 95.44%, clears the national target.

South Australia and Victoria follow at about 94.7%. The Northern Territory is lowest, at 91.53%. Queensland and Western Australia also sit near 92%. Overall, although most states are not at the 95% target, they are above 92%. As the time series shows, this is an improvement from past years.

Rates rose for years, but have started slipping after 2020

National coverage climbed from 2006 through 2020. Age-five coverage increased from approximately 80% in 2006 to a peak of 95.09% in 2020, making it the highest of the three age groups.

By 2024 it had fallen to 93.63%. One-year-olds fell from 94.85% to 92.14%. Two-year-olds fell from 92.55% to 90.44%.

Not every vaccine reaches the same share of children

The heatmap compares coverage across eight vaccine categories for two-year-olds in 31 PHNs. The clearest pattern is the difference between vaccine types: hepatitis B and polio form the strongest columns across most PHNs, averaging about 95%, while the lower-performing vaccines show more pale cells and greater local variation.

Australia has 31 PHNs, so the chart also makes it possible to compare local consistency rather than only the national average. DTP, MMR, and varicella lag near 90-91%, and their columns contain a wider spread of results than hepatitis B or polio. The North Coast and Gold Coast stand out because several vaccines are near 85%, suggesting a broader local shortfall rather than one unusually weak vaccine.

State-level coverage: where has coverage improved, and where does it remain lower?

Every state increased age-five coverage between 2006 and 2024, although the pace and final level differ. The next charts show that change, how rates move from age one to five, and how the Primary Health Networks inside each state compare.

Every state climbed toward 95% over 18 years

South Australia jumped from 76.5% in 2006 to 94.69% in 2024. The ACT now leads at 95.44%, showing that some jurisdictions have reached or passed the national target.

Queensland rose from 82.29% to 92.39%. The Northern Territory rose from 78.8% to 91.53%. Both remain furthest from the 95% target line.

Coverage dips at age two, then recovers by five

In 2024, every state's line slopes down from age one to age two, then turns upward by age five. That shared V-shaped pattern is more important than any single state value: it suggests that the age-two milestone is a common point of missed or delayed vaccination, while later follow-up helps many children catch up.

The size of the dip still varies substantially. Western Australia has the deepest fall, reaching 88.9% at age two, whereas the ACT remains highest across all three ages and finishes at 95.44%. Comparing the slopes shows that some states recover almost fully by age five, while others remain below their age-one level, leaving a persistent gap despite the shared recovery.

Primary Health Networks vary more than states

The PHN map shows a wider local spread than the state-level comparison suggests. Western NSW reaches 96.07% of five-year-olds vaccinated, while the North Coast sits at 88.00% - an eight-point difference within the same national system. Several inland and metropolitan networks cluster toward the higher end, while lower values appear in a smaller group of coastal and regional networks.

Gold Coast (89.12%) and Central Queensland, Wide Bay and Sunshine Coast (89.20%) also sit under 90%. This contrast shows why a state average can hide important local differences: two networks in one state can be farther apart than the overall state rankings. The population slider helps separate broad patterns from small-population results, so comparisons are not driven only by the most visible marker.

Aboriginal children catch up by age five

Nationally, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander five-year-olds now have coverage slightly above the overall five-year-old rate. The remaining national gap is concentrated at ages one and two, while local patterns vary between PHNs.

The gap is at one and two, not at five

In 2024, Aboriginal coverage is 89.9% at age one and 87.74% at age two. All children sit at 92.14% and 90.44%, so the clearest difference appears before age five.

By age five the pattern reverses. Aboriginal children reach 94.95%. All children sit at 93.63%.

A long catch-up, then a shared post-2020 dip

The time-series lines show Aboriginal age-five coverage below the all-children rate until about 2013, followed by a sustained crossover. After that point, the Aboriginal line remains above the comparison line, so the change is better understood as a long catch-up trend than as a one-year fluctuation.

Both groups then peak around 2020 - 97.25% for Aboriginal children and 95.09% for all children - before easing by 2024. The shared downward movement after the peak indicates a broader post-2020 shift affecting both groups, while the continued separation between the lines shows that the earlier catch-up was not erased.

Most networks sit near the diagonal. A few do not.

Points near the diagonal mean similar Aboriginal and overall rates. Regional networks often sit above it, while points below it indicate that Aboriginal children are covered at a lower rate locally.

Northern Sydney is the stark outlier: 86.15% of Aboriginal five-year-olds versus 93.91% of all five-year-olds. Gold Coast and the North Coast show the reverse.

Australia's vaccine supply is international; coverage varies locally

Lines show vaccine flows, not travel paths. The world view is above; the Europe view below locates the main origins.

Australia sources several childhood vaccine products from Europe and the United States. Its DTP3 coverage is high but not the highest among the countries shown, illustrating that manufacturing location and national coverage are different measures.

Supply lines run mainly through Europe

Hexavalent, MMR, pneumococcal, meningococcal, and varicella products used in Australia are made in Belgium, the Netherlands, France, and the United States. The map highlights a concentrated European manufacturing cluster rather than an even global distribution: several coloured routes converge on Europe, while the United States provides a smaller set of separate connections.

Europe contains the main manufacturing cluster around Rixensart, Leiden, Puurs, and Marcy-l’Étoile. Comparing the world map with the Europe detail makes the same pattern easier to read: the broad view shows the geographic reach of the supply network, while the close-up reveals that multiple vaccine routes originate from nearby sites. These are supply links, not evidence that one country supplies every vaccine.

Among supplier countries, Australia is mid-pack

In 2025, 92% of Australian one-year-olds had DTP3 coverage. Belgium is at 98% and France is at 96%, placing Australia below several of the countries connected to its vaccine supply.

The United States is at 94%. The Netherlands, another source of hexavalent vaccine, is at 90%. High local supply does not always mean the highest coverage.
