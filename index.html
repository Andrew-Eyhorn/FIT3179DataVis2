Childhood immunisation in Australia: how close is coverage to the 95% target?

National coverage

Australia's national target is 95% childhood immunisation coverage. The charts follow the story from the national level to states, Primary Health Networks (PHNs), vaccine types, and population groups, showing where progress is close to the target and where gaps remain.

Age-five coverage sits just below the 95% target

In 2024, 93.63% of five-year-olds were vaccinated nationally. The ACT is the only jurisdiction above the target, at 95.44%, showing that the national result is close but not yet evenly reached.

South Australia and Victoria follow at about 94.7%, while the Northern Territory is lowest at 91.53%. Queensland and Western Australia are also near 92%. Most states have moved into the low-to-mid 90s, but the spread shows why a national average can hide uneven progress.

Rates rose for years, but have started slipping after 2020

National coverage climbed from 2006 through 2020. Age-five coverage rose from about 80% in 2006 to a 95.09% peak in 2020, when it became the strongest of the three age groups.

The later decline matters because it affects every age shown: by 2024, five-year-olds had fallen to 93.63%, one-year-olds to 92.14%, and two-year-olds to 90.44%. The two-year-old result is the lowest point in the age pathway.

Not every vaccine reaches the same share of children

A Primary Health Network (PHN) is a local health-planning region that groups nearby communities and services. This heatmap compares the eight vaccine categories in Australia's immunisation schedule for two-year-olds across 31 PHNs, making it possible to see both the national vaccine pattern and local exceptions at the same time.

Hepatitis B and polio form the strongest columns, averaging about 95%, while DTP, MMR, and varicella sit nearer 90-91%. The labelled cells show the contrast: Western NSW reaches 97.16% for hepatitis B, but North Coast is at 85.67% for MMR and 85.93% for varicella. Several low North Coast cells point to a broader local shortfall rather than one unusually weak vaccine.

State-level coverage: where has coverage improved, and where does it remain lower?

Every state increased age-five coverage between 2006 and 2024, although the pace and final level differ. Together, the next charts show the long improvement, the age pathway behind the final result, and the local PHN variation within states.

Every state climbed toward 95% over 18 years

South Australia made one of the largest gains, from 76.5% in 2006 to 94.69% in 2024. The ACT now leads at 95.44%, showing that some jurisdictions have reached or passed the national target.

Queensland rose from 82.29% to 92.39%, and the Northern Territory from 78.8% to 91.53%. Their progress is substantial, but both remain furthest from the 95% line, so improvement has not yet produced equal outcomes.

Coverage dips at age two, then recovers by five

In 2024, every state's line slopes down from age one to age two, then turns upward by age five. This shared V-shape suggests that age two is a common point for missed or delayed vaccination, while later follow-up helps many children catch up.

The size of the dip still varies substantially. Western Australia falls to 88.9% at age two, whereas the ACT remains highest across all three ages and finishes at 95.44%. Some states recover almost fully by age five; others remain below their age-one level, leaving a persistent gap despite the shared recovery.

Primary Health Networks vary more than states

This map shows the local variation that PHNs reveal beyond state averages: Western NSW reaches 96.07% of five-year-olds vaccinated, while the North Coast sits at 88.00% - an eight-point difference within the same national system. Several inland and metropolitan networks cluster toward the higher end, while lower values appear in a smaller group of coastal and regional networks.

Gold Coast (89.12%) and Central Queensland, Wide Bay and Sunshine Coast (89.20%) also sit under 90%. Two networks in one state can therefore be farther apart than the state rankings suggest. Use the population slider to test whether the pattern persists beyond the smallest networks, rather than letting one visible marker dominate the story.

Aboriginal children catch up by age five

Nationally, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander five-year-olds now have coverage slightly above the overall five-year-old rate. The remaining gap is concentrated at ages one and two, while the PHN comparison shows that local experiences are not uniform.

The gap is at one and two, not at five

In 2024, Aboriginal coverage is 89.9% at age one and 87.74% at age two, compared with 92.14% and 90.44% for all children. The clearest difference therefore appears before age five, not at the endpoint.

By age five the pattern reverses: Aboriginal children reach 94.95%, compared with 93.63% for all children. The endpoint reflects catch-up, not a uniform advantage at every earlier milestone.

A long catch-up, then a shared post-2020 dip

The time series shows Aboriginal age-five coverage below the all-children rate until about 2013, followed by a sustained crossover. The lasting separation makes this a long catch-up trend rather than a one-year fluctuation.

Both groups peak around 2020 - 97.25% for Aboriginal children and 95.09% for all children - before easing by 2024. Their shared decline points to a broader post-2020 shift, while the continued separation shows that the earlier catch-up was not erased.

Most networks sit near the diagonal. A few do not.

Each point compares a PHN's Aboriginal five-year-old coverage with its overall rate. Points near the diagonal indicate similar outcomes; points above it have higher Aboriginal coverage, while points below it indicate a local gap.

Northern Sydney is the stark outlier, at 86.15% for Aboriginal five-year-olds versus 93.91% overall. Gold Coast and the North Coast show the reverse pattern, reminding us that the national comparison does not describe every PHN.

Australia's vaccine supply is international; coverage varies locally

Lines show vaccine flows, not travel paths. The world view is above; the Europe view below locates the main origins.

Australia sources several childhood vaccine products from Europe and the United States. The two views below separate where vaccines are made from how well each country covers one-year-olds: related measures, but not the same outcome.

Supply lines run mainly through Europe

Hexavalent, MMR, pneumococcal, meningococcal, and varicella products used in Australia are made in Belgium, the Netherlands, France, and the United States. The routes cluster in Europe, while the United States provides a smaller set of separate connections, so the supply network is international but not evenly distributed.

Europe contains the main manufacturing cluster around Rixensart, Leiden, Puurs, and Marcy-l’Étoile. The world map shows the network's reach; the Europe detail shows several routes beginning close together. These are supply links, not evidence that one country supplies every vaccine.

Among supplier countries, Australia is mid-pack

In 2025, 92% of Australian one-year-olds had DTP3 coverage. Belgium reached 98% and France 96%, placing Australia below several countries connected to its vaccine supply.

The United States is at 94%, while the Netherlands, another source of hexavalent vaccine, is at 90%. The comparison makes the key distinction visible: supply connections do not determine a country's coverage ranking.
